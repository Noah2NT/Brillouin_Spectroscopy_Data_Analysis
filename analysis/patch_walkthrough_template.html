BRILLOUIN RECONSTRUCTION · A WORKED EXPERIMENT

Inside a patch reconstruction

Follow the same measured cell from noisy photons to a predicted spectrum at every pixel. The graphs below contain the actual numerical updates. Select any pixel and compare its entire spectrum across all stages.

1. What are we trying to recover?

An expected spectrum is the average curve we would see over many identical acquisitions. A count cube stores one spectrum at every row and column of the image. The model predicts that cube. In the simulation, the expected curve is known, so we can check mistakes directly.

Poisson noise means the count variance equals the expected count. Brighter bins have greater absolute noise but smaller relative noise. We fit counts before any normalization. A predicted spectrum can include surrounding medium, optical blur and instrumental broadening.

The measurement equation

Y[y,x,b] ~ Poisson(μ[y,x,b]). Y is the observation; μ is the unknown expectation; b is a frequency bin. The reconstruction estimates μ. It does not infer the unblurred, medium-free material curve.

2. Take overlapping patches

A patch is a small square of adjacent pixels, with every spectral bin retained. Move it one pixel at a time. Flatten each patch into a row: first all bins of its first pixel, then the next pixel, and so on. Extraction reorganizes the data; it does not improve a spectrum yet.

Only patches wholly inside the raster are used. Border pixels receive fewer estimates than interior pixels. No reflected copies are treated as additional observations.

3. Group patches with similar spectra

Non-local means patches can share information even when they are far apart. Their coarse spectral shapes decide which group they join. For grouping only, we add consecutive bins into at most 16 bands, retaining spatial positions inside each patch. The final fit still uses every original bin.

A cluster is one group. Its centre is the arithmetic mean of its members. Poisson clustering compares a patch to these centres using the count likelihood. Group labels in the image identify patches by their top-left position; they are not cell-type labels. Grouping itself still leaves the measured cube unchanged.

4. Make a positive initial prediction

A low-rank model describes many curves with a few shared patterns. Here it is the log of the expected intensity that has low rank. A dictionary, V, contains these patterns. Coefficients, U, tell us how much of each pattern a patch uses. We predict exp(UV), so positive intensity is built into the model.

Initialization blends each patch with its group average, adds a small numerical offset, takes a logarithm and keeps a small number of singular-value patterns. This is a starting guess, not the final answer. The subsequent objective uses the unsmoothed measured counts.

Rank is model flexibility. Rank three does not mean three biological materials.

5. Watch the whole cell and every individual spectrum change

One iteration contains two updates. First hold V fixed and adjust U: each patch changes its coefficients. Then hold U fixed and adjust V: the shared patterns change. A Newton step uses slope and curvature of the objective; a line search shortens steps that would increase it.

The stage slider records both halves, not just completed iterations. Each displayed whole-cell prediction is formed by averaging overlapping patches at that stage. Groups are aligned by local update number, although computed one after another; a converged group stays at its final value.

What is the cell map showing?

The default is a spectral centroid: sum(frequency × predicted counts) divided by total counts. This is a convenient summary of the full curve, not a Lorentzian-fitted Brillouin frequency shift. The spectrum panel retains all bins. Other map modes show brightness, change from the raw centroid, and relative shape error where truth exists.

6. Put patches back and average their overlaps

One pixel can belong to several fitted patches, each proposing a slightly different curve. Place every patch back at its original position, add the proposals at each pixel, then divide by the number of proposals. The coverage graph above shows the denominator. Averaging combines estimates; it does not create extra independent photons.

This plot follows the selected pixel above. Thin coloured lines are individual final patch proposals; the thick dark line is their average. Large disagreement indicates sensitivity to patch context, not independent uncertainty.

The last stage is the final average. Overlap averaging already appears in each intermediate image to make the updates comparable. To inspect individual proposals programmatically, walkthrough.npz contains final_patches. Reshape to (valid patch rows, valid patch columns, patch height, patch width, bins). The complete cube history is stored as predictions.

7. Ask what was left behind

A residual is measurement minus prediction. The standardized residual below divides by sqrt(prediction), the model's acquisition-noise scale. Bands or spatial patterns can indicate removed signal or a poor noise model. Near-zero residuals everywhere can indicate overfitting.

The plotted training objective counts overlapping observations more than once. It is useful for checking optimization, but should not be interpreted as independent evidence from each patch. Residuals on the fitted data are also affected by fitting. They are not an unbiased test of predictive accuracy.

8. Results: did the curves actually improve?

For simulated data, the same method is evaluated against a second, independently sampled acquisition. That acquisition and the known truth never enter the fit, initialization or grouping. Parameters are fixed for this walkthrough; they were not tuned on these test photons.

The raw stage is omitted from these two axes so small changes among fitted estimates remain visible. The run has a fixed 12-iteration default budget; a final stage is not a claim of full convergence. A post-hoc minimum on the independent curve is a diagnostic, not a validated stopping rule.

Shape error is the relative Euclidean error after each curve is normalized to unit area. RMSE is root mean square error. Deviance compares predicted counts with independent photons; smaller is better, but noise prevents it reaching zero. Cell-only shape and centroid scores use the simulator's cell mask exclusively for evaluation. Independent deviance includes the whole raster.

The raw comparator uses a small positive floor where measured counts are zero, which can make its deviance large. Compare the denoisers with one another too. The spectrum-only control has one pixel per patch; it shares a spectral dictionary without spatial overlap averaging.

9. Room for improvement

  1. Choose flexibility using separate photons. Use a calibration acquisition to select rank, patch size and number of groups. Reserve independent data for final evaluation.
  2. Test small structures separately. Whole-cell improvement can conceal blurred edges or erased thin features. Compare boundary error and feature contrast with spectral-only reconstruction.
  3. Repeat the experiment. One simulated cell and one seed cannot establish biological reliability. Repeat at shorter exposures, different seeds and different cell geometries. The repository's earlier reconstruction benchmark provides a broader comparison.
  4. Calibrate actual detector data. Gain-scaled intensities, offsets, read noise or resampled bins require a corresponding likelihood. Integer camera values alone do not prove Poisson counts.
  5. Measure uncertainty. Repeated acquisitions or an appropriate bootstrap can assess variability. The square root of a prediction describes acquisition noise, not uncertainty in the reconstructed expectation.
  6. Treat medium separation as another problem. A clean mixed spectrum still contains medium. Additional physical anchors are needed to infer a medium-free cell spectrum.

Training loss may keep improving after predictive accuracy stops improving. If the stage-by-stage truth score worsens while the loss falls, that is a useful demonstration of why smoother output and a better fit are insufficient.

10. Check your understanding

Does extracting a patch denoise its pixels?

No. It reorganizes the measurements so related patches can share a model.

Why not normalize before fitting?

Normalization couples the spectral bins and removes total-count information. Independent Poisson likelihood is then no longer the same measurement model.

Does a falling training objective prove a better biological reconstruction?

No. Independent prediction, boundary preservation and repeatability are separate checks.

Are overlapping patch estimates independent repeats?

No. They reuse many of the same photons. Their disagreement is not a calibrated confidence interval.
